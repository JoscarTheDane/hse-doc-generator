# Chapters 10-12: Decompression Procedures, Medical Emergencies, DCI Treatment

## CHAPTER 10: DECOMPRESSION PROCEDURES & TABLES

### 10.1 Introduction
- Uses **US Navy Diving Manual Revision 7a** decompression tables
- Maximum depth for air dives: **165 feet (50 meters)** — for diving below 50m, closed diving bell used with He/O2 equipment and required personnel on board
- Under normal operations: dives not extended beyond **Repetitive Group 'O'**
- Not extended beyond bottom time limits in **HSE DIV Information Sheet No 5** — any deviation needs approval by Director of Operations
- Surface interval (surface decompression): shall not exceed **5 minutes** from last water stop until diver reaches 50 feet (15.3m) in DDC
- Standby diver required on all surface and wet bell diving operations
- Bail-out bottles charged with appropriate breathing medium to maximum capacity
- **Maximum PPO2 in diver's main surface air/gas supply: no more than 1.4 Bar**

### 10.2 Air Decompression Procedures

**Pneumofathometer Correction Factor:**
- Pneumo reading between depths 0-100 fsw: **+1 fsw correction**
- Pneumo reading between depths 101-200 fsw: **+2 fsw correction**

**General Use of Air Decompression Tables (Table 9-9):**
- Combines three modes of decompression into one table:
  1. In-water decompression on air
  2. In-water decompression on air and oxygen
  3. Surface decompression on oxygen
- Enter table at depth exactly equal to or next deeper than diver's maximum depth
- Select schedule for bottom time exactly equal to or next longer than diver's actual bottom time
- Read across row to obtain required decompression stop times
- Last decompression stop at 20 fsw
- Total ascent time given in next column
- Repetitive group designator upon surfacing given in last column

**In-Water Decompression on Air:**
- Top row labeled "Air" under each depth/bottom time entry gives schedule
- Enter table at depth equal or next deeper than actual dive depth
- Select schedule for bottom time equal or next longer than actual bottom time
- Read across "Air" row to obtain decompression stop times
- Last stop at 20 fsw
- Example: Dive to 78 fsw for 47 min → Enter at 80 fsw, 50 min → 17 min stop at 20 fsw → Repetitive group: "M"

**In-Water Decompression on Air and Oxygen:**
- Bottom row labeled "Air/O2" gives schedule for in-water air/oxygen decompression
- Follow air schedule to 30 fsw (or 20 fsw if no 30 fsw stop), then shift from air to 100% oxygen
- Oxygen stop times shown in **bold print**
- Oxygen stop time begins when all divers confirmed on oxygen
- **If more than 30 minutes on oxygen: 5 min air break every 30 minutes**
- All stops deeper than 30 fsw done on air
- Stops on oxygen commence at 20 or 30 fsw per Table 9-9, in bold type

**Shifting to 100% Oxygen at 30 or 20 fsw:**
1. Supply 100% oxygen to diver
2. Ventilate diver for 20 seconds
3. Verify oxygen monitoring device on dive control panel shows 100% oxygen
- For dives first stop at 40 fsw or deeper: travel/shift/vent time includes 20-second ascent from 40 to 30 fsw plus time to shift, ventilate, confirm
- For dives first stop at 30 or 20 fsw: travel/shift/vent time only includes time to change supply, ventilate, confirm (travel time NOT included)
- Travel/shift/vent time recorded as minutes and seconds, should be **under 3 minutes**

**Air Breaks at 30 and 20 fsw:**
- Diver breathes oxygen for 30 min periods separated by 5 min air breaks
- Air breaks do not count toward required decompression time
- When air break required: change supply to air for 5 min then back to 100% oxygen
- Ventilation of divers NOT required during air breaks
- If total oxygen stop time is **35 minutes or less: air break NOT required at 30 minutes**
- If final oxygen period is 35 minutes or less: final air break at 30-min mark NOT required
- Surface diver on 100% oxygen upon completion of oxygen time

**Surface Oxygen Decompression:**
- Follow in-water air decompression schedule (top row) through end of 40 fsw water stop, then initiate surface decompression
- If no 40 fsw water stop in air schedule: surface diver without taking any stops
- Start timing surface interval when diver leaves 40 fsw
- Required oxygen time in recompression chamber shown in next to last column of Table
- Oxygen time divided into periods — each period 30 minutes long, each half-period 15 minutes long
- **First 15 minutes always spent at 50 fsw in chamber**
- Remainder of oxygen time taken at 40 fsw
- If schedule requires only one half of oxygen period: diver spends 15 min breathing oxygen at 50 fsw, then surfaces at 30 fsw/min

**Surface Decompression on Oxygen Procedure:**
1. Complete any required decompression stops on air 40 fsw and deeper
2. Upon completion of 40 fsw stop: bring diver to surface at **40 fsw/min**
3. If 40 fsw water stop not required: bring diver from bottom to 40 fsw at 30 fsw/min, then from 40 fsw to surface at 40 fsw/min
4. Once on surface: tenders have approximately **3 and a half minutes** to remove breathing apparatus and diving dress and assist diver into recompression chamber
5. Place diver in recompression chamber — chamber operator monitors diver closely for signs of DCI and CNS oxygen toxicity
6. Compress diver **on air to 50 fsw at maximum compression rate of 100 fsw/min**
7. Surface interval = elapsed time from time diver leaves 40 fsw water stop to time diver arrives at 50 fsw in chamber
8. **Normal surface interval should not exceed 5 minutes**
9. **WARNING: interval from leaving 40 fsw in water to arriving at 50 fsw in chamber CANNOT exceed 5 minutes without incurring a penalty**
10. Upon arrival at 50 fsw: place diver on 100% oxygen by mask, instruct diver to strap mask on tightly for good oxygen seal
11. Diver breathes oxygen for number of 30-minute periods and 15-min half periods indicated in next to last column
12. First period: 15 min on oxygen at 50 fsw followed by 15 min on oxygen at 40 fsw
13. Periods 2-4 spent at 40 fsw
14. If more than 4 periods: remaining periods spent at 30 fsw
15. Ascent from 50 to 40 fsw and from 40 to 30 fsw at 30 fsw/min
16. Ascent time from 50 to 40 fsw included in first oxygen period
17. Ascent from 40 to 30 fsw (if required) should take place during air break
18. **Interrupt oxygen breathing with 5-min air break after every 30 minutes on oxygen**
19. This air time is considered dead time — oxygen time begins when diver confirmed on oxygen at 50 fsw
20. When last oxygen breathing period completed: return diver to breathing chamber air
21. Ascend to surface at 30 fsw/min

**Surface Decompression from 30 and 20 fsw:**
- Diving supervisor can initiate surface decompression at any point during in-water decompression at 30 or 20 fsw
- May become desirable if sea conditions deteriorating, diver feels ill, or contingency arises
- May be initiated regardless of whether divers decompressing on air or oxygen
- Supervisor may prescribe full number of chamber oxygen periods listed in table or elect to reduce that number to take credit for time already spent on air or oxygen in water
- If surface decompression elected before divers shifted to oxygen: take full number of chamber oxygen periods prescribed by table
- If surface decompression elected after divers switched to oxygen: compute number of chamber oxygen periods by multiplying remaining oxygen time at stops by 1.1, dividing total by 30 min, then rounding result up to next highest half period
- One half period (15 minutes at 50 fsw) is minimum requirement
- If surface decompression elected while divers decompressing on air: first convert remaining air time to equivalent remaining oxygen time, then compute number of chamber oxygen periods

### Bottom Time Limitations
- **Air diving limited to 165 fsw / 50 msw maximum**
- **Surface orientated air diving total in-water time per 24 hours limited to 4 hours**
- Back-to-back diving requires minimum of two chambers available and sufficient personnel to operate both simultaneously
- All diving time/depth maximum limits per HSE DIV Information Sheet No 5 / IMCA D014

### Ascent Rate
- From bottom to first decompression stop, between decompression stops, from last decompression stop to surface: **30 fsw/min** (20 seconds per 10 fsw)
- Minor variations between 20 and 40 fsw/min acceptable
- For surface decompression: ascent from 40 fsw water stop to surface is **40 fsw/min**
- If rate of ascent **greater than 40 fsw/min: STOP THE ASCENT**, allow watches to catch up, then continue

**Ascent Rate Adjustments:**
- If rate of ascent less than 30 fsw/min causes delay >1 minute reaching first stop:
  - If delay occurs **deeper than 50 fsw**: round up delay to next whole minute, add to bottom time, recompute decompression schedule
  - If delay occurs **shallower than 50 fsw**: round delay time up to next whole minute, add to first decompression stop
- If rate of ascent faster than 30 fsw/min: STOP ASCENT, allow time to catch up, continue at correct speed

**Delays in Leaving Stop or Between Stops:**
- Delay less than 1 minute leaving air stop or between air stops: **disregard the delay**
- Delay greater than 1 minute leaving air stop or between air stops **deeper than 50 fsw**: add delay to bottom time and recalculate
- Delay greater than 1 minute leaving air stop or between air stops **shallower than 50 fsw**: ignore the delay, resume normal schedule
- Delay leaving oxygen stop at 30 fsw or delay between oxygen stops at 30 and 20 fsw: subtract delay from subsequent 20 fsw oxygen stop time
- If delay causes total time on oxygen deeper than 20 fsw to exceed 30 minutes: shift diver to air at 30-min mark
- Delay leaving 20 fsw oxygen stop: can be ignored, but do not leave divers on oxygen longer than 30 minutes
- Delay in travel from 40 fsw to surface for surface decompression: disregard unless diver exceeds allowed 5-minute surface interval

### 10.3 Emergency Procedures

**Bottom Time in Excess of Table:**
- In rare instance of diver entrapment or umbilical fouling, bottom time may exceed longest listed
- If foreseen bottom time will exceed longest listed: immediately contact Diving Safety Department for advice
- If cannot contact in time: read down to deeper depths in Air Decompression Table until depth found that has schedule equal to or longer than bottom time

**Surface Interval Greater Than 5 Minutes:**
- If surface interval more than 5 min but less than or equal to 7 min: increase time on oxygen at 50 fsw from 15 to 30 minutes (add one-half oxygen period), ascend to 40 fsw during subsequent air break
- If surface interval greater than 7 min: continue compression to depth of 60 fsw. Treat on Treatment Table 5 if original schedule required 2 or fewer oxygen periods. Treat on Treatment Table 6 if original schedule required 2.5 or more oxygen periods.

**Decompression Sickness During Surface Interval:**
- If Type I DCI symptoms during travel/undress: compress to 50 fsw following normal procedures
- Delay neurological exam until diver reaches 50 fsw stop and is on oxygen
- If Type I symptoms resolve during 15 min 50 fsw stop AND surface interval ≤ 5 min AND no neurological signs: increase 50 fsw oxygen time from 15 to 30 min, then continue normal decompression
- If Type I symptoms do NOT resolve OR surface interval > 5 min: compress to 60 fsw on oxygen. Treat on Table 5 if original schedule required 2 or fewer oxygen periods. Treat on Table 6 if 2.5 or more.
- If Type II DCI symptoms during travel/undress/neuro exam abnormal: compress to 60 fsw on oxygen. Treat on Treatment Table 6.

**Loss of Oxygen Supply in Chamber:**
- Temporary loss: have diver breathe chamber air, return to oxygen breathing when temporary loss resolved, any time spent on air = dead time
- Permanent loss: complete decompression in chamber on 50% nitrogen 50% oxygen (preferred) or on air
- If 50/50 available: multiply remaining oxygen time by two to obtain equivalent chamber decompression time
- Air breaks not required when breathing 50/50
- If chamber air is only gas: multiply remaining chamber time on oxygen by ratio of water stop times on air at 30 and 20 fsw to oxygen time at those depths to obtain equivalent chamber decompression time on air
- Allocate: 10% to 40 fsw, 20% to 30 fsw, 70% to 20 fsw stops
- Round stop times to nearest whole minute
- If at 50 fsw when loss occurs: ascend to 40 fsw and begin stop time
- If loss at 30 fsw: allocate 30% to 30 fsw, 70% to 20 fsw

**CNS Oxygen Toxicity in Chamber:**
- At first sign of CNS oxygen toxicity: remove from oxygen, allow to breathe chamber air
- **Fifteen minutes after all symptoms completely subsided**: resume oxygen breathing at point of interruption
- If symptoms develop again or first symptom is convulsion:
  - Remove mask
  - After all symptoms completely subsided: decompress 10 feet at rate of 1 fsw/min (for convulsion, begin travel when patient fully relaxed and breathing normally)
  - Resume oxygen breathing at shallower depth at point of schedule interruption
  - If another oxygen symptom after ascending 10 fsw: complete decompression on chamber air

**Asymptomatic Omitted Decompression:**
- If diver shows symptoms of DCI or AGE: immediate treatment using appropriate recompression treatment table essential
- Even if no symptoms: omitted decompression must be addressed to avert later difficulty
- Planned: condition develops at depth requiring surface before completing all decompression stops, time to consider all options
- Unplanned: diver suddenly appears on surface without warning or misses decompression for unforeseen reason

**No Decompression Stops Required — Uncontrolled Ascent:**
- If diver makes uncontrolled ascent to surface at rate greater than 30 fsw/min but dive within no-decompression limits: **diver should be observed on surface for one hour** to ensure DCI or AGE symptoms do not develop
- **Recompression NOT necessary unless symptoms develop**

**Omitted Decompression Stops at 30 and 20 fsw:**
- If diver on surface for less than 1 minute: return to depth of stop from which came, increase that stop time by 1 minute, resume decompression per original schedule
- If diver on surface for 1 to 5 min and chamber available: place in chamber, complete decompression using surface decompression (compute number of chamber oxygen periods per formula above, minimum 1 half period at 50 fsw)
- If diver on surface for more than 5 min but less than or equal to 7 min and chamber available: place in chamber, complete decompression using surface decompression, increase time on oxygen at 50 fsw from 15 to 30 minutes
- If diver on surface for more than 7 min and chamber available: treat with Table 5 if surface decompression schedule required 2 or fewer oxygen periods, Table 6 if 2.5 or more oxygen periods
- If diver on surface for more than 1 min and no chamber available: return to depth of omitted stop, complete decompression in water by multiplying 30- and/or 20-fsw air or oxygen stops by 1.5

**Omitted Decompression Stops Deeper Than 30 fsw:**
- If diver omits part or all of decompression stop at 40 fsw or deeper and chamber available on site: **treat diver with Treatment Table 6**

**Decompression Sickness in Water:**
- Most likely to appear at shallow stops just prior to surfacing
- Some cases occur during ascent to first stop or shortly thereafter
- **Surface the diver for treatment in recompression chamber** — surface interval should be 5 min or less, diver should be considered to have Type II decompression sickness even if symptoms are Type I
- After completing recompression treatment, **observe diver for at least 6 hours**
- If any symptoms recur, treat as recurrence of Type II symptoms

### 10.4 Post-Dive Requirements
Upon completion of each dive, decompression or treatment, diver must:
- Have physical condition checked by Supervisor, LST or Diver Medic (verbal questioning, visual observation)
- Be instructed to report any physical problems or adverse physiological effects (aches, pains, current illnesses, symptoms of DCI or gas embolism)
- Be advised of location of operational decompression chamber
- Be instructed to remain in vicinity of decompression chamber
- Be instructed to obey rules for aircraft travel after diving per DMAC 07

### 10.5 No-Decompression Limits & Repetitive Group Designation Table
- No-Decompression Table (Table 9-7) gives maximum time at given depth without need for decompression stops
- At depths of 20 fsw and shallower: no limit on time
- Deeper than 20 fsw: time limited (e.g., at 60 fsw: 60 min max)
- Even though no decompression stops required, diver still surfaces with residual nitrogen
- This residual nitrogen needs to be accounted for if repetitive dive planned

### 10.6 Repetitive Diving
- When repetitive diving planned: first dive maximum bottom time used is that which puts Diver no higher than **repetitive group "O"**
- Second dive made using bottom time indicated by repetitive group "O" minus penalty
- Diver must not dive again until **12-hour surface period** (note: Chapter 6 says 18-hour for hyperbaric exposure — this section says 12-hour)
- When number of short repetitive no-decompression dives made: shall not exceed bottom time for group letter "O"
- Diver shall not dive again until surface period of 12 hours when group letter "O" is reached
- **Policy: only carry out repetitive diving in emergency project requirement scenario and for maximum of 2 days**
- Where possible, all project dive teams sufficient in numbers to ensure repetitive diving not required

**Repetitive Dive Procedure:**
- Surface interval between dives must be at least 10 minutes
- If surface interval less than 10 minutes: add bottom time of two dives, enter decompression table at deeper of two depths
- To determine decompression schedule when surface interval > 10 min:
  - Obtain repetitive group designator from Air Decompression Table or No-Decompression Table upon surfacing
  - Enter top half of Table 9-8 (Residual Nitrogen Time Table) with repetitive group designator on diagonal
  - Read horizontally to locate time interval that includes diver's surface interval
  - Read vertically down column to obtain repetitive group designator at end of surface interval
  - Continue down same column to depth row equal or next deeper than repetitive dive depth
  - Time at intersection of column and row = residual nitrogen time in minutes
  - Add RNT to actual bottom time of repetitive dive = Equivalent Single Dive Time (ESDT)
  - Enter Air Decompression Table or No-Decompression Table at depth equal or next deeper than actual depth of repetitive dive
  - Select schedule for ESDT, follow prescribed decompression to surface

**Riser Tables:**
- **NOT Atlantic Marine's policy to carry out Riser Table as means of diving operations or decompression method**
- Riser Table offshore diving procedures shall NOT be used
- If specific project requires such dive plan: full review of dive requirement, methodology, experience of personnel, alternative methods in RA before approval from Project Manager and Company Safety Manager

## CHAPTER 11: MEDICAL EMERGENCIES NOT REQUIRING RECOMPRESSION

### General
- In all serious cases and cases of doubt: **medical assistance must be sought immediately**
- Any illness occurring during or soon after dive must be **assumed to be dive-related unless proved otherwise**
- Diver must not remain underwater if not feeling well
- Standby diver must be prepared to enter water and render aid — deployed at first indication of diver stress
- Any loss of consciousness underwater may prove fatal
- Although these emergencies do not generally require recompression, **need to observe decompression schedules must not be overlooked**

### 11.1 Squeeze

**Middle Ear Squeeze (Blocked Eustachian Tube):**
- Signs: severe pain in ear during descent, redness/swelling of eardrums, ruptured eardrum (pain followed by sudden relief, bleeding into middle ear/ear canal, cold water entering may cause severe dizziness/nausea/vomiting)
- Action: stop descent, attempt to equalise, if unsuccessful ascend to shallower depth, if further efforts fail abort dive, if rupture occurs abort dive, wait until symptoms pass, standby diver may assist

**External Ear Squeeze (Reversed Ear):**
- Caused by blockage of external ear canal during descent or ascent (wax, foreign bodies, tight fitting headgear/hood/spider/speakers)
- Avoid tight fitting headgear, keep ear canal free of wax

**Sinus Squeeze:**
- Signs: blood/mucous discharge from nose, pain on ascent if Ostia blocked
- Action: stop descent, ascend slowly to surface, use decongestant nose drops

**Thoracic (Lung) Squeeze:**
- Caused primarily by breath-holding, reduction in breathing medium supply pressure, or sudden fall in water
- Signs: tightness around chest, pain in chest, difficulty breathing after surfacing, bloody frothy sputum
- Action: bring diver to surface (standby may assist), adhere to decompression schedule if possible, alert for need to recompress (may compromise lung decompression/filtering), place in "drainage" position and clear blood from mouth, if breathing ceased administer mouth-to-mouth resuscitation

**Body Squeeze:**
- Caused by non-equalisation of suit or hardhat from failure of gas supply, non-functioning non-return valve, or rapid increase in depth without compensating gas pressure
- **Most serious and potentially fatal**
- Signs: tightness around chest, difficulty inhaling, swelling of tissue in head/neck/shoulders, bleeding into skin/membranes, unconsciousness
- Action: bring diver to surface (standby may assist), adhere to decompression schedule if possible, alert for need to recompress, place in "drainage" position and clear blood from mouth, if breathing ceased administer mouth-to-mouth, apply cold packs to skin bleeding areas, hospitalise after determining no recompression required (unless injury mild)

### 11.2 Respiratory Emergencies

**Oxygen Toxicity — Respiratory System (UPTD):**
- Can occur from inspiration of oxygen at partial pressures greater than 0.5 atmospheres
- Signs: lung irritation causing mid-lung discomfort, cough, pain upon deep inhalation
- Action: reduce partial pressure of oxygen if possible, adapt decompression/treatment schedule
- UPTD = T × (2P - 1)^0.833 where T = time in minutes, P = oxygen partial pressure

**Oxygen Toxicity — Central Nervous System:**
- Signs (VENTID memory device): Vision, Ears, Nausea, Twitching, Irritability, Dizziness
- Most common first symptom: **tingling in fingers**
- Convulsions occur very rapidly in onset
- Action: halt ascent and maintain constant depth, remove BIBS mask at once, protect convulsing patient from injury from contact with hard objects and maintain clear airway until convulsion ceases
- For non-convulsive reactions: have divers hyperventilate for several breaths on lowest safe oxygen mixture available
- Wait 15 minutes after reaction completely subsided, resume schedule at point of interruption
- If using USN Oxygen/Air Saturation Treatment Table 7: wait 30 minutes after reaction completely subsided

**Oxygen Deficiency (Hypoxia):**
- Often no warning signs before unconsciousness
- When present: muscular lack of co-ordination, mental confusion, feeling of well-being (euphoria — very brief), blueness of lips/fingernails (cyanosis), respiratory arrest and death if severe
- Action: have diver stop working, abort dive, send standby diver to assist, switch to secondary/emergency supply, check oxygen concentration in primary supply, have diver ventilate, ventilate chamber if in use, if ascending halt ascent (decrease in pressure diminishes effective partial pressure of oxygen)

**Carbon Dioxide Excess:**
- Caused by inadequate ventilation of apparatus, over-exertion, uncontrolled breathing rate, excessive dead space in equipment
- Signs: headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, mental confusion, lack of co-ordination
- Action: stop working, abort dive, send standby diver to assist, switch to secondary/emergency supply, have diver ventilate, ventilate chamber if in use, check operation of scrubber and condition of soda-sorb if fitted

**Carbon Monoxide Poisoning:**
- Most frequent cause: toxic fumes from compressor exhaust entering air intake
- Reduces blood's capacity to carry oxygen causing tissue hypoxia
- Signs: headache, vertigo/dizziness, pounding of heart, weakness, breathlessness on exertion, confusion/bad judgement/lack of co-ordination, **cherry-redness of lips and fingernails**, respiratory arrest, unconsciousness
- Action: stop working, abort dive, send standby diver to assist, switch to secondary/emergency supply, have diver ventilate, start ascent, ventilate chamber if in use
- If conscious upon surfacing: recompress to 60' and decompress on USN Table 5
- If unconscious upon surfacing or if unconsciousness occurs during recompression on Table 5: **immediately recompress to 165' and treat for gas embolism even if CO poisoning suspected**
- If breathing stopped: initiate mouth-to-mouth resuscitation while recompressing

**Toxic/Irritant Vapour Inhalation:**
- Caused by inhalation of toxic fumes from liquid storage or noxious gases
- Signs: dizziness, headache, nausea, weakness, unpleasant taste/smell, chest pain, coughing, unconsciousness
- Action: stop working, abort dive, send standby diver to assist, switch to secondary/emergency supply, have diver ventilate, ventilate chamber if in use, start ascent if any doubt about diver's condition

**Inert Gas Narcosis:**
- Caused by breathing inert gases at pressure
- Nitrogen narcosis most common (breathing air at depth)
- Signs: confusion, euphoria, lack of co-ordination
- Action: ascend to shallower depth, use less narcotic inert gas (replace nitrogen with helium), minimise level of exertion/cold/CO2, ensure breathing medium provides sufficient oxygen

**Broncho-Spasm:**
- Constriction of air passages by spasmodic contraction of bronchial muscles
- Signs: victim forcibly gasps and wheezes on inspiration and particularly on expiration
- Action: check airway clear, administer pure oxygen if possible, use Duo-medihaler if available (rarely will two or more inhalations required), force fluids, administer mild sedative

**Pulmonary Oedema:**
- Caused by inhalation of toxic fumes, barotrauma, drowning (secondary symptom), left sided heart attack, over transfusion
- Signs: fluid in chest, choking sensation, heavy cough, blueness (cyanosis), possible bloody sputum
- Action: request medical assistance immediately, place victim in position of maximum comfort, administer pure oxygen if possible

**Asphyxiation:**
- Signs: laboured/impaired breathing, headache/dizziness/weakness, unconsciousness with lack of breathing, victim excited/violent while struggling for air, violent increase in breathing followed by cessation
- Action: stop and rest until symptoms appear, begin mouth-to-mouth resuscitation, administer pure oxygen if possible, place victim in uncontaminated atmosphere, if victim in chamber do not decrease pressure until treatment started

### 11.3 Other Emergencies

**Hypothermia:**
- Immersion hypothermia significant loss of body heat in cool/cold water
- Progressive symptoms: shivering (may or may not be present), confusion, lack of co-ordination, loss of consciousness, papillary dilation, cardiac arrhythmia
- Danger of becoming hypothermic without realising extreme danger
- **Treatment:** re-warming, do not remove diving suit, take victim to shower and direct spray to trunk (try not to get hot water on extremities), water temperature between **38 and 44°C**, if bath available leave diving suit on and place victim in bath with extremities outside
- As recovery begins: monitor vital signs
- When noticeable improvement: remove from shower/bath, wrap in blankets for further re-warming
- **Watch closely for recurrence** — release of cold blood from extremities during re-warming can cause dangerous fall in body core temperature
- Provide hot, nourishing beverages
- Effective aid: providing warmed air/gas for inhalation (can be done by mouth-to-mouth resuscitation even if diver breathing spontaneously)

**Vomiting Underwater:**
- Signs: nausea
- Action: send standby diver to assist, abort dive, start ascent, follow decompression schedule if possible, upon surfacing check airway and administer mouth-to-mouth if necessary, evacuate to medical assistance and monitor for secondary drowning symptoms

**Underwater Blast Injury:**
- Pain due to perforated eardrums, internal injury (chest, stomach, sinus regions), inner ear barotrauma
- Action: abort dive, send standby diver to assist, start ascent, follow decompression schedule if possible, observe closely for injuries, do not ignore apparently minor injuries (serious hidden internal damage may be present), diver must be returned to shore for medical examination

**High Pressure Water Jet Injury (DMAC 03):**
- Wound may appear insignificant and give little indication of extent of injury beneath/damage to deeper tissue
- Initial mild damage to wall of organ may result in subsequent rupture, particularly if infection introduced
- Development of subsequent infection particularly important in abdominal and joint injuries
- Outcome depends on extent of initial injury and presence/absence of infection
- Even if injury seems trivial and patient has no complaints: **great importance to arrange for surgical examination ASAP**
- Where surgical examination not immediately possible in remote situation: first aid confined to dressing wound and observing closely for further complaints over 4-5 days. Development of fever and rising pulse rate suggests serious injury, together with persistence/occurrence of pain.
- Where surgical advice unobtainable: consider providing course of antibiotic therapy
- Action: abort dive, send standby diver to assist, start ascent, follow decompression schedule if possible, observe closely, do not ignore apparently minor injuries, diver must be returned to shore for medical examination

**Injury from Marine Animals:**
- Biting/cutting wounds from shark, barracuda, moray eels, coral formations, barnacles, mussels, stingrays
- General treatment: abort dive, adhere to decompression schedule if possible, keep diver at rest, remove foreign material from wound, wash wounded area continuously with clean fresh water, administer pain relief drugs (danger of masking DCI symptoms), administer booster injection of tetanus toxoid if possible
- Stingray wounds: soak for 30 minutes in water as hot as can be tolerated, use anaesthetic/anti-histamine/cortisone ointments
- Puncture/stinging wounds from sea urchins, jellyfish, cone shells, venomous fish, sea snakes:
  - If small puncture: make small incision at site to promote bleeding and allow flushing of wound
  - Wash copiously with fresh water
  - Apply weak ammonia or baking soda solution if available
  - Administer pain relief drugs if necessary (danger of masking DCI symptoms)
  - Use anaesthetic/anti-histamine/cortisone ointments
  - **Depending on degree of toxicity: considerable delay between attack and onset of symptoms possible**
  - **Personnel suffering attack must be closely monitored for at least 24 hours**

**Weil's Disease (Leptospirosis):**
- Caused by direct contact with urine of infected animal (e.g. rat) or indirectly through water contaminated by that urine
- **Do not dive in polluted waters without using dry diving suit including gloves and full mask or rigid helmet. Avoid contact by any part of skin with polluted water.**
- First phase (ten days after exposure): fever, headache, red eyes, aching muscles, extreme skin tenderness, jaundice, lip sores
- Second phase (three days after first phase): fever, headache, neck stiffness, light sensitivity
- **Treatment:** within four days of onset, can be treated with antibiotics. Bed rest and general medical supervision needed.

**Jellyfish Stings:**
- Most dangerous: Portuguese man of war and sea wasp
- Avoid contact (even with detached tentacles) by wearing rubber suit
- Signs: pain at site (mild like nettle sting or severe enough to cause unconsciousness), redness/swelling/blistering/loss of body sensation, muscle cramps, abdominal pain, paralysis of limbs, nausea/vomiting, severe backache
- **Treatment:** get out of water, remove remaining tentacle/sting fluid with stick or cloth, wash copiously with water, apply weak alkali (saturated solution of baking powder), treat for shock

**Bilharziasis (Schistosomiasis):**
- Carried by minute parasitic worms entering body when diving in polluted waters
- Common in Middle East, Far East, South America
- **Do not dive in polluted waters without dry diving suit including gloves and full face mask or rigid helmet**
- Incubation: skin rash with itching
- Acute stage (one month later): fever, abdominal tenderness, diarrhoea, jaundice, blood in urine
- Chronic stage (years later): malaise, gastrointestinal symptoms, blood in urine, nervous disorders
- **Treatment:** specific chemotherapy kills adult worms

**Sea Urchin Stings:**
- Penetration by sharp barbed spines or introduction of venom by small stone-like pincers
- Clothing does not afford full protection — contact must be avoided if possible
- **Treatment:** remove spines/pincers with forceps (surgical removal may be required)

**Venomous Fish and Sea Snake Stings:**
- Venomous fin spines capable of inflicting potentially lethal injuries
- Found in warmer waters around pipelines and platforms
- After contact: immediate and intense pain with swelling which may progress to axilla or groin
- **Treatment:** immediate treatment imperative, keep patient quiet, apply pressure dressing above bite, treat with heat, Anti-serum must be given intravenously (doctor should give polyvalent anti-serum containing Krait fraction, slow intravenous injection), treat for shock

**Cone Shell Stings:**
- **Treatment:** keep patient quiet, apply tourniquet above bite and keep injured area as cold as possible (immerse in ice cold fresh water), treat for shock

**Coral Cuts and Stings:**
- **Treatment:** wash copiously with water, apply weak alkali (saturated solution of baking powder), apply sterile dressing, if general reaction treat for shock

**Alternobaric Vertigo:**
- Unequal middle air equilibration during descent or ascent, particularly during ascent, caused by asymmetrical clearing of middle ear
- Signs: vertigo, nausea, vomiting, unconsciousness
- Action: stop diver, maintain constant pressure, use normal procedures to attempt to clear ears, resume schedule at point of interruption **5 minutes after reaction completely passed**
- If decompressing in chamber and depth less than 60 feet: change breathing medium to pure oxygen

**Bleeding:**
- Signs: blood flowing from wound, progressive increase in pulse rate, progressive skin shutdown (victim cold, pale and clammy)
- Action: apply pressure over wound — use clean cold cloth if available but do not wait, **maintain pressure for at least 5 minutes**
- Compress main artery to wounded area if necessary
- **Tourniquets not to be used unless wound catastrophic (severed limb)** — if required, just tight enough to stop bleeding and no more, otherwise soft tissue damage may occur. Tourniquet pressure must be released momentarily every **15 minutes**.
- Keep wounded area at rest, apply clean dressing
- **Pressure Points:** if severe bleeding from open wound of arm or leg does not stop with direct pressure and elevation, may need pressure point (in addition to, not in place of, direct pressure)
  - **Brachial Artery:** located on inside of upper arm in groove between biceps and triceps, about midway between elbow and armpit. Grasp middle of victim's arm, thumb on outside and fingers on inside, press fingers toward thumb to create inward force. Use flat inside surfaces of fingers, not fingertips.
  - **Femoral Artery:** located on front, center part of diagonally slanted "hinge" of leg, in crease of groin area where artery crosses pelvic bone. Position victim on back, place heel of hand directly over pressure point, lean forward over straightened arm to apply pressure. Keep arm straight to prevent strain. If bleeding not controlled, may compress directly over artery with flat of other hand.

**Tourniquet Procedure (if all other methods failed and life in danger):**
- Place tourniquet **above wound**, not allowing it to touch wound edge
- If wound in or just below joint area: apply tourniquet immediately above joint
- Wrap band tightly around limb twice, tie half knot
- Place short strong stick in overhand knots on top of stick
- Twist stick to tighten until bleeding stops
- Secure stick in place with loose ends of tourniquet
- **Make note of location of tourniquet and time it was applied, attach to victim's clothes**
- Once applied, should not be loosened except on advice of physician
- **Do not cover a tourniquet**

**Cardiac Arrest:**
- Respiratory accidents, shock or electrocution may cause heart to stop
- Circulation of blood ceases, oxygen no longer provided to body/brain — permanent brain damage can occur in **less than 3 minutes**
- Signs: unconscious victim, cessation of breathing, no pulse, pupils dilated/no response to light, blueness (cyanosis)
- Conscious victim: severe pain, shortness of breath, dilated pupils, apprehension, restlessness, shock
- **Treatment:** request medical assistance immediately, place victim on back in outstretched position on hard surface with head back to maintain clear airway, if breathing stopped start artificial resuscitation, if no pulse present use CPR, often CPR can restart heart (substitutes for action of heart muscles, pumping blood through body until heart takes over)
- Mouth-to-mouth artificial resuscitation required simultaneously with cardiac compression

**Shock:**
- May occur with any injury, certainly present to some extent in any serious injury
- Result of loss of circulatory blood, drop in blood pressure and decreased circulation
- Signs: breathing shallow/irregular/laboured, eyes glassy/lacklustre/pupils dilated/tired looking, blueness in lips/fingernails, pale/ashen grey skin, pulse may be normal/weak/rapid, wet/clammy/cold skin, drop in blood pressure, possible retching/vomiting/nausea/hiccups, thirst
- **Treatment:** ensure adequate breathing, control bleeding if present, administer pure oxygen if possible, elevate lower extremities, avoid rough handling, prevent loss of body heat (wrap in blanket under as well as on top — do not attempt to warm with hot water bottles or aggressive measures, do not allow victim to become overheated), give nothing by mouth, remove cause of pain if possible

**Heat Exhaustion:**
- Occurs when cardiac output and vasomotor control cannot meet increased circulatory demands of skin in addition to brain and muscles
- Usually caused by simultaneous exposure to heat and very hard work
- Signs: rapid weak pulse, nausea/vomiting, fainting, restlessness, headache, dizziness, difficulty breathing (rapid/shallow), cold/clammy skin/continuous sweating
- **Treatment:** place victim in shaded/cool place with head lower than rest of body, protect from chilling, have victim drink weak salt water (1 teaspoon salt in 1 gallon of water)
- Recovery usually fairly rapid, but symptoms (headache/exhaustion) may linger — observe carefully until fully recovered

**Heat Stroke:**
- Prolonged exposure to heat raises body temperature — serious emergency, body temperature must be quickly lowered to prevent permanent brain damage or death
- Signs: rise in body temperature, sudden collapse, skin extremely hot and dry, convulsions, breathing deep at first then very shallow, extremely strong and rapid pulse, dilated pupils
- **Treatment:** completely immerse body in cool water if possible (sponge head and neck with same cool water), if body cannot be immersed bathe continually with cool water, have victim drink weak salt water (1 teaspoon salt in 1 gallon of water)
- Upon recovery: victim should be examined by physician or other qualified medical personnel

**Near Drowning:**
- Usually result of hypoxia followed by asphyxia caused by loss/failure of air supply, panic, pain, fear or other stressful condition
- Term "near drowning" used when victim recovered from water unconscious and not breathing
- May be determined immediately that victim not suffering from gas embolism — but if any doubt, treat as if gas embolism
- Signs: unconsciousness, blueness in skin/lips/fingernails, cessation of breathing
- **Treatment:** immediate artificial resuscitation, request medical assistance
- **COLD-IMMERSED VICTIM MAY ONLY APPEAR DEAD** — recoveries reported after victims spent as long as 40 minutes underwater. CPR should be administered and continued while victim being re-warmed.
- Interaction of foreign fluid in lungs and lungs' own reaction to it can cause secondary drowning symptoms and severe pulmonary oedema
- **All near drowning victims should be closely monitored for at least 24 hours after revival**

**Resuscitation of Drowning Victims:**
- Mouth-to-mouth or mouth-to-nose artificial respiration should be started at once in any case where breathing has ceased
- **Only after artificial respiration initiated and heart stopped should external heart massage be started and combined** (CPR)
- CPR techniques:
  - **Mouth-to-mouth ALWAYS started first**, then necessity for external heart massage determined
  - Place victim on back, kneel beside victim's shoulder
  - Clear victim's mouth and air passage of foreign objects, loosen clothing
  - Place one hand under victim's neck (head-tilt-chin-lift), do not touch neck if broken neck suspected (apply jaw thrust instead)
  - Extend airway by lifting jaw forward
  - Place other hand on victim's forehead so thumb and forefinger can pinch nose
  - Lift gently with hand under neck while pushing down with hand on forehead
  - Initially: give victim two breaths without interruption, watch for chest to rise
  - As soon as chest rises: remove mouth from victim's and allow air to expire naturally
  - Repeat 12-14 times per minute for adults, 18-20 times for children and infants
  - If chest does not rise: check for air leak (airtight seal) or airway obstruction (insert finger in mouth, remove foreign objects, vomit, blood clots)
  - For adults: apply chest compression only with one hand on top of other, thrust forcefully into upper abdomen to compress lungs and expel residual air (foreign object often pops out like cork from bottle)
  - For children: roll infant over forearm, give sharp blow between shoulder blades
  - If chest still fails to rise: remove hand from neck, insert thumb into victim's mouth, grab lower jawbones between thumb and finger, lift jawbone upward
  - Mouth-to-nose breathing may be carried out using similar techniques, mouth closed while mouth placed over nose
  - Mouth-to-mouth may be carried out through handkerchief if hesitant (airways and tubes should NOT be used — dangerous when used by untrained personnel)

**External Heart Massage (CPR Compressions):**
- After artificial respiration instituted with 2 quick breaths, **only then** check if external heart massage should be started
- Needed only if heart has stopped
- Check pulse at carotid in neck (large artery close to surface on either side of Adam's apple)
- Check pupils — if dilated and do not constrict when light hits them, blood flow to brain insufficient
- If no pulse and/or pupils dilated and do not constrict: start external heart massage
- Victim must be on firm surface (ground, spine board, floor)
- Locate notch at top of breastbone, locate lower end of breastbone — **GREAT CARE not to place hand over tip of breastbone (xiphoid process)**
- **Measure two finger-widths up from Xiphoid Process** — place heel of one hand over lower one-third of breastbone, other hand on top of first

**CPR for One and Two Rescuers:**
- **Single rescuer:** interrupt external heart massage every **30 beats** to give **2 lung inflations**. Each series of 30 compressions at about 100 per minute. Two inflations in quick succession within period of 5 seconds. **DO NOT allow full lung exhalation between breaths.**
- **Two rescuers:** **2 lung inflations and 5 chest compressions** (30:2 ratio)
- One positioned at victim's head (administer artificial respiration, monitors pulse at carotid without interrupting respiration)
- Other on opposite side of victim's body at shoulder level (external heart massage)
- Bring shoulders directly over victim's breastbone, keep arms straight, rock back and forth slightly from hip joints, exert pressure vertically downward to depress lower breastbone
- **Release pressure immediately** — compression and relaxation must be of equal duration
- **Do not remove heel of hand from victim's chest when pressure is released**
- Be sure pressure completely released so breastbone returns to normal resting position between compressions
- Breastbone compressed **1.5 to 2 inches for adults**
- For small children: heel of one hand only
- For infants: tips of middle and index finger only
- Compress over mid-sternum for small children and infants (heart lies higher in chest)
- Cycle repeated 100 times per minute in adults and children, smooth rhythmic fashion
- Keep fingers away from victim's ribs to avoid fractures (fingers may be interlocked)
- Check pulse frequently to see if heart has started
- **Cardiostomach distension:** may interfere with lung inflation, may indicate blocked airway — remedy by applying pressure over stomach with palm of hand (may also lead to regurgitation, be ready to turn victim's head to one side and clean out mouth)
- **CPR, once started, must be continued until spontaneous breathing and heartbeat occur or until victim turned over to physician or emergency medical services personnel** — must be continued while victim being transported to medical facility
- **Under no circumstances should CPR be interrupted for more than 5 seconds**

**Automated External Defibrillator (AED):**
- **Atlantic Marine policy to have an AED on each work site**
- Suitably trained personnel (Diving Supervisor / Diver Medic) responsible for storage, maintenance and use
- Two forms: Automated and Semi-automated
- Specialized computer that recognizes heart rhythms requiring defibrillation
- Rationale: patient in ventricular fibrillation needs to be defibrillated within **2 minutes** to ensure survival
- **AED Procedure:**
  1. Assess responsiveness, airway, breathing & circulation
  2. Initiate CPR while waiting for AED to arrive
  3. When AED available: stop CPR, turn on AED, apply pads, analyse rhythm
  4. If no shock advised: continue/start CPR
  5. If shock advised: deliver shocks
  6. Check pulse and breathing after shock delivered
  7. If patient begins breathing: give oxygen or place on recovery position
  8. If patient not breathing: assist ventilation
  9. If no pulse: continue CPR for 1 minute, re-analyse, deliver more shocks if prompted
  10. Repeat sequence until professional medical help arrives

**AED Safety:** Make sure electricity injures no one, do not defibrillate patient lying in pooled water, dry soaking wet patient's chest first, do not defibrillate someone in contact with another person or conducting material

**AED Maintenance:** read operator's manual, check AED and battery every day, get checklist from manufacturer, report failures to manufacturer

**Medical Direction:** Medical Doctor approved protocol, periodic review of AED usage, review speed of defibrillation, refresh skills every 3-6 months

## CHAPTER 12: MEDICAL EMERGENCIES REQUIRING RECOMPRESSION

### Introduction
- In ALL cases (or suspected cases) of DCI: **Company diving doctor is to be contacted**
- Initial recompression should be commenced as quickly as possible, **shall NOT be delayed while waiting for advice**
- Diving Supervisor makes initial diagnosis and starts treatment, only then contacts diving doctor with complete history of events and symptoms
- During any DCI treatments: injured diver accompanied inside chamber by another diver (preferably Diver Medic)
- Where possible and when time allows: neurological examination prior to recompression for baseline
- **However, recompression should not be delayed for this purpose** — brief examination can be carried out, complete neurological exam to be completed upon arrival at treatment depth
- **Atlantic Marine uses latest revision Decompression, Recompression and Treatment Tables from US Navy (Revision 7)**

### Treatment Tables
- US Navy Oxygen Treatment Tables (6 and 6A) proven very effective, treatment procedure of choice
- Air treatment tables not as effective, used only when dictated by necessity
- **DO NOT USE USN Treatment Table 4, 5, 7 or 8 UNLESS DIRECTED BY DIVING DOCTOR QUALIFIED IN HYPERBARIC MEDICINE**
- Occasional serious cases of DCI or gas embolism necessitate protracted recompression — in those cases or where oxygen treatment tables ineffective: advice from qualified diving doctor shall be followed
- Incidence of blow-up, free ascent, omitted decompression always treated by recompression
- **Initial treatment of any DCI shall be by placing patient in DDC to 60 fsw and breathing 100% oxygen**

### When Requesting Emergency Assistance
- Always ensure information on how person contacted can return the call
- Be prepared to provide phone numbers, email addresses, MARSAT information, radio call signs
- Once appropriate doctor contacted: every attempt to get him in direct contact with Diving Supervisor
- All other personnel in communications loop keep informed by communicating with physician
- Diving Supervisors must be familiar with ERP to obtain assistance in area of work
- **A call should be placed at start of diving contract to ensure necessary communications can be established**
- **BE PREPARED — no time to be looking for phone numbers etc. when suddenly faced with emergency**
- Use prefix **"DIVING MEDICAL EMERGENCY"** in all radio/telephone communication regarding diving incident

### Decompression Pulmonary Barotrauma
- Syndrome resulting from gas normally retained within airways leaving natural confines and entering either interstitial space, pleural cavity or bloodstream
- Signs: sharp chest pain usually behind breastbone, shortness of breath, difficult/painful breathing, cough (may be productive of slightly blood-stained sputum)

**Arterial Gas Embolism (AGE):**
- Most serious result of pulmonary barotrauma
- Gas dispersed from alveolar gas into pulmonary venous system, carried to heart then into arterial systemic circulation
- Gas bubbles expand with further decreases in pressure, increasing severity
- Clinical features may occur suddenly or preceded by dizziness, headache, great anxiety
- Feeling of being "hit in chest" during ascent may be experienced
- Unconsciousness, cyanosis, shock, convulsions follow quickly (generally within 10 minutes of surfacing)
- Convulsions may be severe and recurrent, may require heavy sedation
- Motor and sensory deficits in various degrees/distributions
- Death from coronary/cerebral occlusion with cardiac arrhythmia, respiratory failure, circulatory collapse, shock
- Physical examination may reveal: air bubbles in retinal vessels, blurred vision, sharply defined area of pallor on tongue, marbling of skin, weakness of extremities, focal/generalised convulsions, large areas of abnormal sensations, other neurological abnormalities
- **Temporary obstruction of air passage (cold or bronchitis) increases risk of gas embolism — diving with chest cold or bronchitis should be avoided**
- **Only effective treatment of embolism is recompression**
- Patient should be kept in head-down position (helps keep bubbles from reaching brain and forces smaller bubbles through brain capillaries)
- Placing patient on left side helps maintain cardiac output
- In non-fatal cases: residual paralysis, myocardial necrosis and other injuries may occur if delay in initiating therapy even if injuries treated correctly
- Neurological symptoms/signs after hyperbaric exposure should be termed **Acute Neurological Decompression Illness** (not AGE)

**Mediastinal and Subcutaneous Emphysema:**
- Gas escapes into interstitial tissue space, tracks along outside of airways and blood vessels to middle of them and into mediastinum
- Small amount of gas often symptomless; substantial amount: mild to moderate retrosternal pain, sensation of fullness in chest/throat, change in tone of voice/hoarseness
- Gas may migrate into subcutaneous tissues of neck (and occasionally head) — not usually painful, may only be detected by swelling or crepitation (crackling sensation of skin)

**Pneumothorax:**
- Alveolar gas escapes into pleural space
- Often painless but may cause sharp pain worsened by deep breath
- Small pneumothorax: few physical signs
- Large pneumothorax: short breath, slight bluing of lips/nail beds, reduced movement of chest wall on affected side, tracheal shift towards affected side, hyper-resonant percussion note, reduced breath sounds
- Not life threatening (can survive with one intact lung), most unusual for both lungs involved simultaneously
- Occasionally leak such that gas escapes into pleural space with each breath but unable to return to lung — gas volume gradually increases = tension pneumothorax

**Tension Pneumothorax:**
- Dangerous — gas continues to escape from perforated lung, pressure generated within chest may eventually cause both lungs to collapse
- Tracheal shift AWAY from affected side
- Cyanosis becomes pronounced, shock/unconsciousness/death may ensue unless treated appropriately
- Simple pneumothorax at depth may increase in size during decompression and become tension pneumothorax
- If diver's condition deteriorates during ascent, especially if symptoms respiratory: **pneumothorax should always be suspected**

**Treatments:**
- Asymptomatic mediastinal/subcutaneous emphysema: usually resolve gradually without specific treatment. If troublesome symptoms: resolution accelerated by breathing 100% oxygen on surface. In very rare instances where serious symptoms: recompression may be necessary. If no associated pneumothorax: safe to compress to 30 fsw for an hour breathing 100% oxygen.
- Small pneumothorax: can be treated by breathing 100% Oxygen on surface
- Large pneumothoraces and all tension pneumothoraxes: require draining — large bore chest drain inserted into chest by appropriately trained individual

### Fitness to Return to Diving After Decompression Illness (per DMAC 13)

- **Limb pain or non-specific manifestations only** (e.g. persistent headache, excessive fatigue, loss of appetite, nausea): Return to diving only after review by diving medicine specialist
- **All other neurological or pulmonary symptoms**: 3 months — return to diving only after review by diving medicine specialist
- **Cutaneous and lymphatic manifestations** without neurological involvement (skin rash with severe itching or marbling/cutis marmorata or swelling of tissues): 28 days — return to diving only after review by diving medicine specialist
- **Pulmonary barotrauma** resulting in pneumothorax or mediastinal/subcutaneous emphysema: Following appropriate investigation including HRCT of chest, diver may be considered fit to return to diving but **no earlier than 3 months after complete recovery** — return to diving only after review by diving medicine specialist
- **In cases where there are residual neurological manifestations even after repeated treatment**: diver should be considered **unfit for occupational diving**
- **In all cases: company diving specialist doctor shall be consulted**

### Therapeutic Decompression

Two types of decompression illness:
- **Type I (Mild)**: pain, itching, sometimes skin blotchy mottled rash or red spots, skin may look like marble, fatigue important symptom of mild DCI may precede serious problems
  - Typical example: itching/burning of particular area of body, may spread and later reduce, tingling of skin
  - Deep pain in or near joint (progressively worse until unbearable) — distinguish between this and muscle/joint sprain (usually painful to touch, swollen and discoloured)
- **Type II (Serious)**: extremely urgent and dangerous
  - Shock, nausea, hearing difficulties, weakness/paralysis/numbness of limbs (frequently follows abdominal pain), dizziness, pain in chest, shortness of breath, extreme fatigue, collapse, unconsciousness
  - Most common signs: sensory and muscular changes of scattered nature affecting one or more limbs
  - Easy to be misled when obvious/painful symptom masks another which may develop into more serious symptom later

### Decompression Illness — Terminology
- New descriptive system replaces Type I/II dichotomy
- Described using **evolution and manifestation terms**

**Evolution (development of condition BEFORE recompression):**
- **Progressive**: number or severity of symptoms/signs increasing ("rapidly" or "slowly" refers to period of minutes or hours respectively)
- **Static**: neither severity nor number of manifestations changing substantially
- **Spontaneously Improving**: substantial improvement without recompression (must be substantial, not transient)
- **Relapsing**: cases that have improved spontaneously then undergo secondary deterioration — reserved for cases that have at some stage undergone substantial spontaneous improvement

**Manifestations:**
- **Limb Pain**: deep aching pain in or around one or more joints, begins gradually and poorly localised, may resolve spontaneously ("niggle"), flits from joint to joint, if worse becomes more localised (dull, boring ache similar to toothache), pain seldom made worse by movement, weight bearing poorly tolerated on affected lower limb. No objective signs on exam (occasionally skin rash over/adjacent to affected joint), NOT redness/swelling/warmth/tenderness (classical signs of inflammation MISSING). Even straightforward limb pain must be FULLY EXAMINED — patient in pain may not notice mild paresthesia or small area of numbness. Ensure no neurological symptoms before choosing therapeutic table. Resolves completely even without treatment over 12-72 hours.
- **Girdle Pain**: poorly localised aching or "constricting" sensation in abdomen/pelvis/chest — generally considered ominous since frequently portends neurological deterioration
- **Neurological**: subtle, multi-focal, bewildering variety. Higher functions (aberration of thought processes/affect, loss of memory, dysarthria), alteration of level of consciousness including seizures, loss of co-ordination, loss of strength/sensation with almost any distribution, dysfunction of special senses, loss of sphincter control (especially bladder). May involve brain or spinal cord. Short deep dives with rapid ascent most likely to provoke predominantly spinal cord distribution. Onset commonly occurs shortly after reaching surface, about half of serious cases symptomatic within 10 min, less than 10% present more than 4 hours after completing dive. Severe cases: onset heralded by girdle pain, pins and needles/numbness/muscular weakness in legs rapidly progresses to paraplegia. May involve all four limbs, shock may complicate picture. In less severe cases: onset not dramatic, progress to paraplegia may be delayed and incomplete. "Level" often in lower thoracic or upper lumbar segments. Bladder frequently involved (difficulty initiating urination or absence of urinary output and distended bladder on exam). Unless rapidly treated with recompression, complete recovery unlikely.
- **Audio-Vestibular**: vertigo (sense of rotation), tinnitus, nystagmus or loss of hearing after dive. Nausea/vomiting may accompany but by themselves insignificant. Recompression does not appear to have adverse effect on pathology due to round or oval window rupture. All such cases should be referred for specialist investigation to establish need for perilymph fistula.
- **Pulmonary**: chest pain, cough, haemoptysis, shortness of breath, cyanosis, rarely shock. Progressive disease may be due to tension pneumothorax or massive gas embolism of lungs.
- **Cutaneous**: severe itching around shoulders or over trunk, develops into erythematous rash which may progress to cyanotic mottling or marbling of skin
- **Lymphatic**: lymph nodes enlarged and tender, associated oedema, skin feels thickened with "pitted" appearance (orange peel), pressure applied then released after about minute leaves visible indentation
- **Constitutional**: headache, fatigue, malaise (including nausea and possibly vomiting), anorexia

**Additional Information for DCI Cases:**
- **Time of Onset**: DCI usually presents within short period after dive. Most serious neurological or pulmonary symptoms manifest within about 30 min. Limb pain may be delayed for many hours. DCI may be provoked or made worse many hours after dive if diver takes flight. If asymptomatic for **48 hours or more after dive and has not flown**: symptoms developing subsequently probably not dive-related.
- **Gas Burden**: record dive profile as accurately as possible (from dive computer or depth-time recorder if available)
- **Evidence of Barotrauma**: record any evidence of barotrauma (middle ear, pulmonary, audio-vestibular)
- **Response to Recompression**: important to record
- **Results of Investigations**: tests for PFO, electrophysiological tests, perfusion scans of brain — important part of case notes

**Descriptive Diagnostic Label Examples:**
- "ACUTE, RELAPSING, NEUROLOGICAL, DECOMPRESSION ILLNESS"
- "ACUTE, PROGRESSIVE LIMB PAIN AND CUTANEOUS DECOMPRESSION ILLNESS"
- In rare highly complex cases: "multi-system"

### Rules for Treatment of Decompression Illness

**ALWAYS:**
- Contact Company diving doctor
- Follow treatment tables accurately
- Have qualified tender diver in chamber at all times during recompression (preferably DMT)
- Maintain normal descent and ascent rates
- Examine patient thoroughly at depth of relief or treatment depth
- Treat unconscious patient for gas embolism or serious DCI unless possibility can be ruled out without question
- Use oxygen if available
- Be alert for oxygen toxicity if oxygen used
- In event of oxygen convulsion: remove oxygen mask, keep patient from harming himself, **DO NOT force open mouth during convulsion**
- Maintain oxygen usage with time and depth limitations
- Check patient's condition before and after coming to each stop and during long stops
- Observe patient for at least **6 hours after treatment for recurrence of symptoms**
- Maintain accurate time keeping and recording
- Maintain well-stocked medical kit at hand
- Ensure patient remains within few minutes of decompression chamber for at least **24 hours after completion of treatment**

**NEVER:**
- Permit any shortening or other alteration to tables except under direction of qualified medical personnel
- Exceed descent rate tolerated by patient
- Let patient sleep between depth changes or for more than one hour at any one step
- Continue ascent if patient's condition worsens
- Wait for bag resuscitator — use mouth-to-mouth immediately if breathing ceases
- Break rhythm during resuscitation
- Permit use of oxygen at depth greater than 60 feet
- Fail to report symptoms early (diver)
- Fail to treat doubtful cases
- Allow personnel in chamber to assume any cramped position which may interfere with complete blood circulation

### Recompression Treatment Tables Summary

**Treatment Table 6 — MANDATORY if:**
- Type I pain is severe and immediate recompression must be instituted before neurological exam can be performed
- Complete neurological examination cannot be performed
- Any neurological symptom is present
- These rules apply no matter how rapidly/complete symptoms resolve once recompression begins
- Also used for: Type II symptoms, AGE responding to treatment at 60 feet, Type I symptoms not showing complete relief within 10 min after arriving at 60 feet
- Table 6 extended at 60 feet if residual symptoms (other than mild soreness around affected joints) persist after third oxygen breathing period
- If residual symptoms other than soreness persist at 30 feet: extend at 40 feet
- AGE also treated by initial compression to 60 fsw. If symptoms stabilize within first oxygen breathing period, treatment continued using Table 6. If during first oxygen breathing period deterioration noted: compression to 165 feet should begin and Table 6A started. If Company diving doctor on scene/available: treatment may continue at 60 feet on his recommendation.
- Also used following diver blow-up (from depths greater than 20 fsw) when diver asymptomatic but more than 30 min of decompression omitted, and for all symptomatic divers who blow up or surface with omitted decompression from 60 fsw or shallower.

**Treatment Table 6A — Used to treat:**
- AGE or decompression symptoms when severe symptoms remain unchanged or worsen within first 20 minutes at 60 fsw
- Patient compressed to depth of relief (significant improvement), **not to exceed 165 fsw**
- Once at depth of relief, begin treatment gas (NITROX, HELIOX) if available
- If shift from Table 6A to Table 4 contemplated: Company diving doctor to be consulted before shift

**Treatment Table 4 — Treatment of:**
- Certain cases of AGE and Type II DCI at 165 feet
- Deterioration noted after initial recompression to 60 feet and/or felt deeper recompression needed
- Cases which have had treatment initiated promptly with no improvement and deterioration occurring at 60 feet
- If more than 6-8 hour delay prior to initiation of recompression: patient probably will not benefit from further recompression to 165 feet — continue treatment at 60 feet
- If deterioration noted during ascent to 60 feet: treat as recurrence of symptoms
- If possible, Company diving doctor to be consulted before committing to Table 4
- If diver put on Table 4: full 120 minutes should be spent at 165 feet unless operational or unforeseen medical considerations dictate earlier decompression
- If oxygen available: patient should begin oxygen breathing periods immediately upon arrival at 60 foot stop
- Breathing periods: 25 minutes on oxygen interrupted by 5 minutes air (each cycle lasts 30 min — simplifies time keeping)
- Immediately upon arrival at 60 feet: minimum of four oxygen breathing periods (total 2 hours) administered
- After that, oxygen breathing to suit patient's individual needs and operational conditions
- Both patient and tender must breathe oxygen for at least 4 hours (eight 25-minute oxygen, 5-minute air periods), beginning no later than 2 hours before ascent from 30 feet is begun
- Both oxygen breathing periods may be divided up as convenient, but at least 2 hours of oxygen breathing periods should be completed at 30 feet

**Treatment Table 5 — Used for:**
- Type I (pain only) symptoms when complete neurological examination has revealed no abnormality
- Asymptomatic omitted decompression from shallow surfacing (20 fsw or less)
- Asymptomatic omitted decompression from blow-up (deeper than 20 fsw) if missed decompression < 30 minutes
- Asymptomatic divers who have exceeded surface interval limits following SurDO2 dive
- After arrival at 60 fsw: neurological exam to ensure no overt neurological symptoms (weakness, numbness, incoordination) present
- If any abnormalities found: diver treated using Treatment Table 6
- In initial evaluation: delay in treatment for up to **15 minutes after DDC ready** permissible to wait for notified medical personnel to arrive. After medical personnel arrive, delay may be extended additional **15 minutes** so adequate neurological exam can be performed. If obvious neurological symptoms occur at any time: diver considered Category A and recompression to 60 feet on Table 6 must commence at once. Even if pain only symptom, do not delay recompression more than **30 minutes** from time DDC ready.
- If complete relief of Type I symptoms not obtained within 10 min at 60 feet: Table 6 required
- Symptoms of musculoskeletal pain showing absolutely no change after second oxygen breathing period at 60 feet may be due to orthopaedic injury rather than DCI. If after review of patient's history, diving medical officer feels pain can be related to specific orthopaedic trauma/injury: Table 5 may be completed. If no Company diving doctor on site: **Table 6 shall be used**.
- If doubt as to presence of neurological or cardio-respiratory symptoms: **Table 6 should be used**. Treating Type II DCI on Table 5 could result in recurrences and substantial residual damage.

**Treatment Table 7 — "Heroic measure" for:**
- Non-responding severe gas embolism or life-threatening DCI
- Extension at 60 feet of Tables 6, 6A or 4 (or any other non-standard treatment table)
- **Considerable treatment has already been administered**
- **NOT designed to treat all residual symptoms that do not improve at 60 feet — should NEVER be used to treat residual pain**
- Should be used only when severity of symptoms such that marked residual impairment or loss of life may result if currently prescribed decompression from 60 feet is undertaken
- Involves isolating patient and ministering to medical needs in DDC for **48 hours or longer**
- Experienced medical and/or paramedical personnel should be on scene
- **Company diving doctor to be consulted before shifting to Table 7**
- Careful consideration must be given to life support capability

**Table 7 Indications:** Continued complete paralysis of limbs, coma and/or loss of spontaneous respiration. NOT used for: numbness, tingling, decreased sensation to touch (paresthesia), limb weakness (as long as patient can move limb against gravity), bladder problems without limb paralysis.

**Time at Depth (Table 7):** Minimum **12 hours** at 60 feet, including time spent at 60 feet from Table 4, 6 or 6A. Do not begin decompression from 60 feet for at least 12 hours. At completion of 12-hour stay: decide whether to decompress or spend additional time at 60 feet. If no improvement during first 12 hours: benefit from additional time at 60 feet unlikely, decompression should be started. If patient improving but significant residual symptoms remain (limb paralysis, abnormal/absent respiration): additional time at 60 feet may be warranted. While actual time at 60 feet unlimited, additional time beyond 12 hours determined by diving medical officer based on patient's response to therapy and operational factors. When patient has progressed to point of consciousness, can breathe independently and can move all extremities: decompression can be started and maintained as long as improvement continues. Solid evidence of continued benefit should be established for stays longer than 18 hours at 60 feet.

**Decompression (Table 7):** Tendrs breathe chamber atmosphere. Chamber oxygen kept above 19% and CO2 below 1.5% surface equivalent. Decompression begun with upward excursion from 60 to 58 feet at time zero. Subsequent two feet upward excursions at time intervals appropriate to rate of decompression. Upon arrival at 4 feet: decompression stopped for 4 hours. At end of 4 hours at 4 feet: decompress to surface at 1 foot per minute. This procedure prevents inadvertent early surfacing.

**Oxygen Breathing (Table 7):** Patients should begin oxygen breathing periods as soon as possible at 60 feet. Oxygen breathing periods of **25 minutes on 100% oxygen followed by 5 minutes breathing chamber atmosphere**. Normally four oxygen breathing periods alternated with 2 hours continuous air breathing. In conscious patients: this cycle continued until minimum of eight oxygen breathing periods administered (previous periods may count against eight). Beyond that: continued as recommended by Company diving doctor as long as improvement noted and patient tolerates oxygen. If oxygen breathing causes significant pain on inspiration: discontinued unless significant benefit from oxygen breathing obtained. In unconscious patients: oxygen breathing stopped after maximum of 24 oxygen breathing periods administered. Actual number and length of oxygen breathing periods adjusted by Company diving doctor to suit individual patient's clinical condition and response to oxygen toxicity.

**Sleeping, Resting and Eating (Table 7):** At least two tenders available when using Table 7, three may be necessary for severely ill patients. All tenders not required to be in chamber — may be locked in and out as required following appropriate decompression tables. Patient may sleep at any time except when breathing oxygen below 30 feet. While asleep: patient's pulse, respiration and blood pressure should be monitored and recorded at intervals appropriate to condition. Food and fluid intake maintained throughout treatment period.

**Life Support (Table 7):** Before committing to Table 7, all life support considerations must be addressed. **Do not commit to Table 7 if internal chamber temperature cannot be maintained at 85°F (29.4°C).**

**Abort Procedures (Table 7):** In some cases Table 7 may have to be terminated early. If extenuating circumstances dictate early decompression and less than 12 hours elapsed since treatment began: decompression may be accomplished using appropriate 60-foot air decompression table as modified below. 60-foot air decompression tables may be used even if time spent between 60 and 165 feet (e.g. on Table 4 or 6A) as long as at least three hours have elapsed since last excursion below 60 feet. If less than three hours have elapsed or if any time was spent below 165 feet: use air decompression table appropriate to maximum depth attained during treatment. All stops and times in air decompression table should be followed but oxygen breathing periods started for all chamber occupants as soon as depth of 30 feet reached. All chamber occupants continue oxygen breathing periods of 25 minutes on 100% oxygen followed by 5 minutes on air until total time breathing oxygen is one-half or more of total decompression time.

**Treatment Table 8 — Adaptation of Royal Navy Treatment Table 65:** mainly for treating deep blowups when more than 60 minutes of decompression missed. Upon recommendation of Company diving doctor, Table 8 can be used for recompression deeper than 165 feet (225 feet maximum) or to extend holds at depths between 165 and 60 feet. Table schedule from 60 feet same as Treatment Table 7.

### Diver's Initial Neurological Examination

**Preliminary Examination of Life-Sustaining Functions (within 15 seconds if chamber treatment required):**
- Breathing: Is chest moving? Count breaths per minute. Place ear on patient's back just below shoulder blades, check for sounds in chest (gargling, wheezing, air rushing through pipe). If coughing: type of cough, frequency, pain/blood/phlegm. Cold or cough before accident?
- Heart: Feel for pulse (neck or groin). Take pulse rate per minute (do NOT use thumb).
- Hemorrhage: Stop any bleeding with direct pressure or apply tourniquet.
- Shock: low blood pressure, pallor, cold/clammy skin, cyanosis, weakness, lightheadedness, thirst, sweating, tachycardia. Check lips and fingernails for abnormal colour. Check pupils for dilation. Does patient report dizziness, pain, ringing in ears? How does patient look in general?

**Neurological Examination (if time permits after stabilization at treatment depth):**

**Mental Status:** Determined from first seeing patient — alertness, orientation, thought process. Determine if patient knows time and place, can recognize familiar people and understands what is happening. Is mood appropriate? Memory intact? Questions: "What is name of your supervisor?", "What did you have for dinner?" Must know answer to questions asked. If cognitive problem: assess more fully — patient should be asked to remember something ("red ball, green tree and couch"), spell word backwards ("world"), count backwards from 100 by sevens, recall information from beginning of examination.

**Coordination (Cerebellum/Inner Ear):**
- Heel to Toe Test: tandem walk, look straight ahead, walk straight line placing heel of one foot directly in front of toes of opposite foot. Look for: does patient limp? Does patient stagger or fall to one side?
- Romberg Test: with eyes closed, feet together, arms extended to front, palms up. Note if patient maintains balance or immediately falls to one side. Some examiners recommend small shove from either side with fingertips.
- Finger to Nose Test: eyes closed, head back, arms extended to side, bend arm at elbow touch nose with extended forefinger alternating arms. Extension: with eyes open, alternately touch nose with fingertip and touch examiner's fingertip (examiner changes position each time), speed not important, accuracy is.
- Heel to Shin Test: standing, touch heel of one foot to knee of opposite leg (foot pointing forward), run heel down shin to ankle, each leg tested.
- Rapid Alternating Movement Test: slap one hand on palm of other, alternating palm up then palm down. Any exercise requiring rapidly changing movement will suffice. Both sides tested.

**Extremity Strength (0-5 grading):**
- 5 = Normal (equal strength bilaterally, able to resist examiner)
- 4 = Mild weakness (able to resist slight force of examiner)
- 3 = Moderate weakness (able to overcome gravity but not resistance of examiner)
- 2 = Severe weakness (able to contract muscle but cannot move joint against gravity)
- 1 = Profound weakness (flicker or trace of muscle contraction)
- 0 = Paralysis (no motion possible)

Upper extremity muscle groups tested: Deltoids, Latissimus, Biceps, Triceps, Forearm muscles, Hand muscles (each compared to opposite limb, examiner provides resistance).

Lower extremity strength: watch patient walk on heels for short distance, then on toes, then "duck walk" (walk while squatting). These assess lower extremity strength as well as balance and coordination.

### Decompression Illness Treatment Record
- Neurological exam form to be completed
- Details of dives within last 72 hours
- History: principle manifestations with time (24 hour clock) and date of onset
- Examination: location of sensory abnormalities recorded (light touch, pin prick, temperature sensation, vibration, proprioception)
- Treatment narrative: time to relief of symptoms/signs and whether complete or partial, problems during treatment (including details of any transfer to alternative tables), patient's condition at end of initial treatment and progress during any re-treatment
